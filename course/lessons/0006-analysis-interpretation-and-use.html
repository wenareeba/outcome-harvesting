    Outcome Harvesting, lesson 6 of 8

    Analysis, interpretation and use: from outcomes to answers

    Steps 5 and 6 turn a set of credible outcome statements into answers the users can act on, and then help them act. The harvester stops mainly facilitating and starts judging. Five sections, five categories to test, eight questions and one paragraph to write.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can set categories that pass the book's four tests, write an interpretation that answers a prime question from the outcome data, and say which kinds of post-harvest support a harvester should and should not offer.

      1From facilitator to evaluator

      By Step 5 the harvester holds a set of outcome statements credible enough to serve as evidence. The work now has two parts (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 104):

      • Analysis organises the raw statements into clusters. The book uses the word strictly: "the process of separating something into its constituent elements in order to clarify the meaning of the whole" (p. 104).
      • Interpretation asks what the clusters mean and produces evidence-based judgements that answer the prime questions (p. 112).

      Who makes those judgements depends on the exercise. In monitoring, developmental and some formative evaluations, the harvester mostly facilitates the users' own judgements. In an external summative evaluation, the harvester answers the questions (pp. 113 to 114). Moving between those roles is hard, and users who owned every outcome may find it difficult when sense-making is taken out of their hands (p. 126).

      The brief adds that analysis can work at three levels: a single outcome rich enough to tell as a story, the outcomes of one change agent, or patterns across many change agents in a larger program (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, pp. 21 to 22).

      2Classify with categories that earn their place

      A small set can be organised by hand, on a table top or a slide. One World Bank team arranged its outcomes along a timeline, grouped by type, with lines showing which outcomes led to others (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 104 to 105). Beyond a few dozen outcomes, or more than about half a dozen ways of classifying them, use a database; the book uses Excel (p. 106).

      Categories can come from the prime questions, or from the intervention's strategy or theory of change where that helps answer them. Either way, try them on the actual outcomes, because unexpected outcomes often suggest categories no one planned (p. 106). The book calls this the spirit of grounded theory: categories built from the data rather than data forced into categories (p. 127, note 2). Classify the descriptions of significance and contribution as well as the outcomes (p. 109).

      TestWhat the category must do
      DistinctDiffer clearly from every other category. An outcome may fall in two categories, but categories must not overlap each other
      DefinedBe spelled out in as few words as possible, to the satisfaction of users and harvesters
      MeaningfulMake sense to both users and harvesters
      UsefulHelp answer at least one prime question, as the questions stand now

      Paraphrased from Wilson-Grau (2019, p. 107).

      The common failure is too many categories. In one evaluation, users spent a month producing 125 categories and ran out of time before testing them; tested against the data, half were enough (p. 108). A category with few outcomes can still stay if the kind of change it captures is important (p. 109). The book leans towards classifying each outcome by the one category it mainly belongs to, and leaving connections for the interpretation (p. 112).

      Which test does each category fail?

      Two WHO categories in one set: "Local government" and "District councils".

      A WHAT category called "Empowerment", with no definition.

      For a question about vocational education, "Girls" split into babies, preschool, primary, secondary and postsecondary ages.

      "Type 3B change: meso-level institutional shift", defined in the harvester's own terms. The users do not recognise it.

      "Funded by the second donor", well defined, in a harvest whose only question is about changes in district policy.

      Categories invented for practice, except the third, which is the book's. The judgements follow the pages cited in each answer.

      3Interpret: answer the questions, and say so

      The book's most important advice on interpretation is to "make sure, one way or another, the harvest questions are answered, even when they are implicit" (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 114). Its worked answer to one question (Box 6.2) opens with a judgement, then supports it cluster by cluster, citing outcome numbers in brackets and naming what the intervention did (pp. 115 to 117).

      The book's nine pointers for interpreting (pp. 118 to 119), in brief:

      1. Question your own reading of the sources, and your own baggage.
      2. Keep the users' principal uses in view for every question.
      3. Explain only the findings that merit it; not all the data has to be used.
      4. Write the first draft as if it were final.
      5. Write in the first person, which owns the judgement.
      6. Do not become a hostage to numbers; persuade with reasoning.
      7. Keep facts and opinions apart: the outcome and contribution are facts as the sources know them, the significance is opinion.
      8. Compare only what can be compared, and remember that the data show only what sources reported.
      9. Cite the key numbers in the text, because many readers skip the charts.

      The lens can be the users' own, such as their theory of change or strategy, or the harvester's expertise, as when grantees' outcomes were read against knowledge of the movement to democratise global institutions (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, pp. 23 to 24). The book shows a second lens on the same outcomes, the Gender at Work framework, and advises asking users before applying one (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 119 to 121).

      Users can make sense of the outcomes together. In one evaluation, 15 network staff and program leaders spent four days arranging 422 outcomes into chains linking activities to outcomes over five years. The evaluator found this collective sense-making the most useful moment of the harvest for the organisation itself (pp. 113 to 114).

      4Report for use, and offer points for discussion

      The book adapts six lessons from Patton on reporting: use is a process, not a report; be intentional about each report's purpose; stay focused on the users; protect credibility; use visuals, with narrative; and take report writing seriously from the terms of reference onwards (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 121 to 123). Its draft outline gives most of the weight to the answers:

      SectionWeight
      Background5%
      Harvest design: users, uses, questions5%
      Harvest methodology, with strengths and weaknesses5%
      Key findings and conclusions, question by question75%
      Conclusions and implications10%

      From Wilson-Grau (2019), Figure 6.4, p. 123. Annexes hold the terms of reference, tools and the outcome database.

      On credibility, the book draws a line. In an external evaluation, the evaluators correct factual errors and rewrite anything unclear, but may keep their interpretation when users disagree; users can respond formally through a management response (p. 122).

      Not quite

      The outline has no recommendations section, on purpose. Decisions turn on political, legal, financial and ethical factors that are often confidential and unknown to the harvester (pp. 123 to 124), a point the brief also makes (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 25). After a recommendation of his once damaged a federation's relationship with its donors, the author advises that harvesters "propose recommended points for discussion" instead, usually as questions (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 124 to 125).

      5Step 6: support use after the report

      The book holds harvests to the standard of actual use: "A harvest that is useable falls short; the harvest must be actually used to have value" (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 131). It calls this step perhaps the most difficult, because commissioners tend to see a harvest as finished once findings are reported (pp. 126, 131).

      Support can be for the findings, the process or the method itself (p. 132). For the findings, the book accepts four tasks and declines a fifth (pp. 134 to 136):

      • Accepts: presenting findings in other formats; further analysis with new categories; reinterpreting through other lenses, such as the theory of change; bringing in others' views, such as participants'.
      • Declines: recommending what action to take.

      Process use often appears during the harvest itself, when staff see that what they called outcomes were outputs, and it can grow into an internal monitoring practice (pp. 138 to 139). In one practitioner's account the book reproduces, a program's field office went on collecting outcome data after the evaluation, and its quarterly reports gave outcome statements a bigger place (Figure 7.3, p. 137).

      RiskWhat it looks like
      Symbolic purposeA harvest commissioned for publicity, funding or delay, with no intent to use it. The author has declined such contracts
      MisuseUnknowingly using the findings of a poorly done harvest
      AbuseKnowingly using faulty findings, or deliberately not using sound ones because they are unwelcome
      Non-useSound findings that decision makers did not need
      OveruseGiving limited findings too much weight, such as scaling up "best practices" where cause and effect will change

      Summarised from Wilson-Grau (2019, pp. 140 to 142), who adapts Alkin (1990) and Patton (2015).

      To manage these risks the book adapts nine follow-up lessons from Patton, among them planning and budgeting for follow-up from the design step, adapting findings for each audience, and guarding against misuse (pp. 142 to 145). One heading sums up the stance: "Champion Use of the Findings, but Do Not Become a Champion for the Intervention" (p. 145).

      PracticeWrite the answer to a prime question

      An invented harvest, January 2025 to April 2026

      Prime question: To what extent did our safe-water advocacy influence government actors, and what does it mean for our strategy?

      1. March 2025: District A council approved a budget line for borehole repairs.
      2. June 2025: District B water office began publishing monthly data on working boreholes.
      3. September 2025: District A council doubled the repair budget line in its budget revision.
      4. November 2025: District C council rejected a repair budget motion after the project's petition was seen as partisan. Negative.
      5. January 2026: District B's water engineer adopted the community fault-report hotline.
      6. February 2026: The national water directorate cited District A's budget line in its guidance to districts.
      7. March 2026: Two water user committees in District B began collecting fees.
      8. April 2026: District A began quarterly review meetings with water user committees.

      The sources describe outcome 6 as the most significant.

      Over 16 months, I find the advocacy influenced government most clearly in District A, where the council moved from approving a repair budget line [1] to doubling it [3] and then opened quarterly reviews with water committees [8]. The national directorate then cited that budget line in its guidance [6], which the sources consider the most significant outcome; that is their judgement, and I report it as such. In District B the changes were in practice rather than budgets: published borehole data [2] and a fault hotline [5]. Outcome 7 is a change by committees, not government. District C shows a risk: the council rejected a repair motion after the petition was seen as partisan [4]. In all, six of eight outcomes are changes by government actors, and one is negative. Because 2026 covers only four months, I do not compare the two years. Points for discussion: what made District A different, and should petitions be run with allies seen as non-partisan?
