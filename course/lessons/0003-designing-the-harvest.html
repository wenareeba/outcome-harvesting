    Outcome Harvesting, lesson 3 of 8

    Designing the harvest

    A harvest is shaped by the people who will use it. This lesson covers Step 1: agreeing who the users are, what they will use the harvest for, the few questions it must answer, and what it will cost. Five sections, an estimator to try, eight questions and one set of questions to draft.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can agree a harvest design with its users: who they are, their uses, two to five prime harvesting questions, the sources, and a first estimate of the time it will take.

      Answer from memory before you read on.

      1Design is six agreements, made with the users

      Step 1 produces a harvest plan. Users and harvesters agree, in principle, on six things (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 30):

      1. the primary intended users;
      2. their principal uses, of the findings and of the process;
      3. the prime harvesting questions;
      4. the outcome information needed to answer them;
      5. the sources, documents first and then people, and how to reach them;
      6. the money, people and time the harvest needs.

      The approach is adapted from Patton's utilization-focused evaluation, which the book calls the method's main methodological pillar (p. 30). Agreements are provisional. Plans for the later steps are rarely more than tentative (p. 31), and users and harvesters keep checking that the work still fits the uses as results emerge (p. 150).The book advises commissioners to involve the harvester in writing the terms of reference, and to recruit an evaluator rather than choose between fixed proposals, because a harvest is always customised (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 201 to 202).

      2Primary users are the people who will act on it

      A primary intended user needs the harvest to make a decision or take action, and takes part from start to finish (p. 31). The book separates them from secondary users, who need the findings but are not involved throughout, and from the audience, who are simply interested (p. 32). Each primary user should be able to answer yes to three questions (p. 31):

      1. Is the harvest, as designed, worth doing for the resources and the uses?
      2. Are we ready to put in the time and effort, alone and as a group?
      3. Are we committed to using the results?

      Too many primary users make decisions slow. A small steering group is one way to keep them manageable (p. 32).

      3Uses come in two kinds: the findings and the process

      The findings can serve accountability to donors, decisions to change course, planning, or engaging allies and funders (p. 34). The process has uses of its own: staff who learn to identify and formulate outcomes, a shared view of what the strategy is achieving, a stronger learning culture (pp. 33 to 34).

      Deciding what the harvest will not serve matters as much. Measured against the five OECD-DAC criteria, the book places Outcome Harvesting (p. 35):

      CriterionHow well OH serves it
      EffectivenessMore appropriate
      RelevanceMore appropriate
      SustainabilityMore appropriate
      ImpactLess appropriate
      EfficiencyLess appropriate still

      Wilson-Grau (2019, p. 35). Where impact or efficiency questions must be answered, the book suggests a different method for them.

      4Two to five questions, each asking what and so what

      The prime harvesting questions guide everything else, much as a hypothesis guides research (p. 34). When users list more than two to five, the book asks them to reprioritise (p. 35). Its working question to users is "What do you need to know and why will it be useful information?" (p. 35).

      A good prime question asks for description and for judgement: what happened, and what it means. The book pairs them (Figure 2.2, p. 36):

      What?So what?
      Which policy and practice outcomes did the organisation influence since a given year?Are those outcomes in line with its strategic plan?
      Which of its activities influenced them?What are the strengths and weaknesses of the strategies that contributed?

      Paraphrased from Wilson-Grau (2019, Figure 2.2, p. 36).

      Vague questions usually get vague answers (p. 35). Test each question against four criteria: the users want it answered and can say how they will use the answer; it can be answered well enough to support action; it needs outcome data; and it can be answered in time and at a reasonable cost (pp. 36 to 38). The book's example, "What has our program done to pursue our strategies?", fails the first and third: the answer is already in reports, and it is not about outcomes (p. 36).

      5Then agree the sources and estimate the time

      Documents come first, then people. The best human sources have first-hand knowledge of the outcomes and how the intervention influenced them, and are motivated to share it (pp. 42 to 43). The design should also settle confidentiality: whose identity needs protecting, and what must stay private in sensitive advocacy work (p. 43).

      The book gives its own rules of thumb for time, and stresses they are estimates (p. 44). Try them on a harvest of your own.

      Estimate harvester time for Steps 1 to 5
      Document review speed
      StepDaysBasis

      Rules of thumb from one experienced harvester (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 44 to 47); 8-hour days. The starting values are the book's own example. Its text gives five pages an hour for document review, but its worked example and Table 2.4 use seven (p. 45), so both are offered.

      Not quite

      The book names the commonest design mistake as setting out to do too much: too many users, uses, questions and sources (p. 160). Its fourth principle answers it: do only as much as necessary to answer the prime questions usefully (p. 159).

      PracticeDraft the prime questions

      Case, from Lesson 1 A foundation has funded a coalition of twelve civil society groups in Uganda for six years to push for better management of public water points. The board must decide in six months whether to fund three more years. The coalition's coordinator wants to learn which of its strategies work. Name the primary users, then draft two or three prime harvesting questions.

      Primary users. The foundation's programme officer, who will brief the board, and the coalition coordinator. The board is a secondary user; member groups are part of the audience and the sources.

      1. Which changes in what district officials, councils and water committees do did the coalition influence between 2020 and 2026, and how far do they match its aims?

      2. Which of the coalition's strategies contributed to those outcomes, and what are their strengths and weaknesses?

      3. Given those outcomes, what should a further three years focus on?
