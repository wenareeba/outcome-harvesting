07 / 08Outcome Harvesting

Critiques and responses: where the method is open to challenge

Every method has weak points, and a harvester who knows them can defend a harvest honestly and design around them. This lesson sets four critiques against the responses in the literature, and says what each response leaves open. Five sections, six objections to judge, eight questions and one reply to a sceptic.

By the end of this lesson

You can state the main critiques of Outcome Harvesting fairly, give the strongest available response to each, and say when a question needs another method.

      01What the critical literature is, and is not

      Earlier lessons rested on the method's own texts. This one draws mainly on others: two peer-reviewed case studies, from Calgary and from England; a methods guide from INTRAC; and a comparison with the Qualitative Impact Protocol (QuIP) by James Copestake, who designed and piloted it. Each is listed under "Read next".

      The base is thin. Abboud and Claussen note "limited exploration in the academic literature supporting OH" (Abboud and Claussen 2016, p. 53), and the sources gathered for this course include no systematic review of the method. Treat what follows as the critiques found in these sources, not a settled verdict.

      Two of the sources have no printed page numbers. INTRAC (2017) is cited by the page of its PDF. Copestake's blog post (2017) and the BetterEvaluation page (Wilson-Grau et al. 2024) are cited by section.

      02Critique: the outcomes are what interested people choose to report

      The sources in a harvest are usually the intervention's own staff. INTRAC puts the risk plainly: outcomes are captured only if someone documented them or the change agent knows of them, and "This might mean a bias towards outcomes that are easy to identify, and away from those that are more difficult to measure" (INTRAC 2017, p. 4). Abboud and Claussen report that rigour and bias were raised throughout their project, and that "the objectivity of collaborative evaluation has been questioned" (Abboud and Claussen 2016, p. 53).

      Copestake's comparison is sharper. QuIP added blindfolding, the separation of data collection from analysis, and systematic coding specifically to reduce bias. "OH has less to say in this regard, and perhaps therefore less to offer, but thereby is more open" (Copestake 2017, after the comparison table).

      Responses. The book's answer is procedural: sources go on record knowing they can be checked, the harvester coaches every statement to SMART, and outcomes can be substantiated by independent people (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 88 to 89, 184). It asks for negative outcomes in their own rows and reminds sources who report none (p. 178). BetterEvaluation adds open-ended questions, rather than only confirming outcomes already documented (Wilson-Grau et al. 2024, step 3), and capturing expected changes that did not happen (advice section). Abboud and Claussen argue, citing others, that the access to and quality of information gained likely outweigh the potential for bias (Abboud and Claussen 2016, p. 53).

      What remains. The book itself concedes that the subjectivity of internal sources "may undermine credibility" (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 184), and it finds negative outcomes consistently hard to harvest (p. 177). Two further questions follow from its own procedure. Substantiators are usually named by the sources whose statements they check, and only the harvester's judgement guards their independence (pp. 93 to 94). And a count of outcomes shows only what sources reported, not what happened (p. 119), so it cannot be read as a measure of how common a change was.

      03Critique: plausible contribution is weak causal evidence

      The book is candid about what its contribution claims are. The causal links are plausible and reached by inductive reasoning: "They are not statistically defensible relationships", and so "the conclusion is likely, but not guaranteed" (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 181). Its test borrows from detective work: did the intervention have a motive, the means and the opportunity to influence the change (p. 182)?

      Responses. The method does not claim attribution. Copestake reads this as a strength shared with QuIP: both seek credible evidence of contribution without estimating precisely how much of an outcome is due to an activity, and he suggests that aiming for too much precision can obstruct a broader and more reliable account of causes (Copestake 2017, before the comparison table). Abboud and Claussen found the method helped practitioners avoid overstating causal contribution through single, linear causal paths (Abboud and Claussen 2016, p. 52).

      What remains. If users need to know how much of a change the intervention caused, or whether it was efficient, the book itself points to another method (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 35). Lesson 8 looks at which methods.

      04Critique: it costs more time and skill than it seems

      The evidence here is consistent across sources:

      • Writing good outcome statements is hard. INTRAC says it takes much time and skill, often needs outside help, and is harder for staff working outside their first language (INTRAC 2017, p. 4). In the Calgary pilots, where practitioners drafted alone after training, only one of six outcomes was complete (Abboud and Claussen 2016, pp. 51 to 52).
      • Records may not hold what is needed. The Calgary teams found their notes covered troubleshooting rather than outcomes, and concluded that the "right" kind of documentation matters (p. 52).
      • Harvests can be large. INTRAC cites one with nearly 200 outcomes and a report of about 400 pages (INTRAC 2017, p. 4).
      • Budgets slip. One retrospective harvest planned for 10.5 days took about 14 (Beardmore, Jones and Seal 2023, p. 3).

      Responses. Harvests can be scaled: BetterEvaluation points to the principle of striving for less, and to harvesting periodically to spread the load on informants (Wilson-Grau et al. 2024, resources section). The English team ran its harvest on a minimal budget in three months and judged the method well suited to small, retrospective evaluations (Beardmore, Jones and Seal 2023, pp. 3, 6). They also argue that most of the challenges they met are common to all evaluation, "not unique to OH" (p. 6).

      What remains. The cost falls partly on informants and staff, not only the harvester. A design that ignores that will produce thin statements.

      05Critique: whose harvest is it?

      The method is participatory for staff and users. INTRAC notes it is "less clear how wider stakeholders (such as social actors) should participate", and warns that data collection can become extractive (INTRAC 2017, p. 4). In Calgary, asking participants to act as substantiators raised questions of role clarity and power (Abboud and Claussen 2016, p. 51). In England, some social actors declined to take part, and one key organisation was underrepresented (Beardmore, Jones and Seal 2023, p. 5).

      Responses. When the people who changed are brought in, the harvest improves. One Calgary participant asked beneficiaries to substantiate her statements; they called them "too simple", and the rewritten outcome captured change she had missed (Abboud and Claussen 2016, p. 52). The book describes users later deciding to include beneficiaries in interpreting what the outcomes mean for their lives (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 135).

      What remains. Social actors' participation is an option the harvest design must choose, not a default of the method.

      Watch the definition

      Sources do not agree on what counts as an outcome. The book treats changes in knowledge, attitudes and skills as outputs unless they show in behaviour (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 175). BetterEvaluation's first content principle includes "attitude, beliefs, or behaviour" (Wilson-Grau et al. 2024, step 2). A harvest should state which definition it uses, because critics and users will read its counts through it.

      PracticeReply to a sceptic

      An invented email from a board member

      "Your harvest is a collection of success stories told by our own staff. Why should the board trust it when we decide on the next phase?"

      You are right that most of the information came from our own staff, and that is the method's main weakness. Three things limit it. Every statement had to name who changed what, when and where, so each one can be checked, and staff knew it would be. We asked separately for negative outcomes, and two of the 41 are negative. And we asked independent people about the 12 outcomes that matter most to the board's decision; 11 confirmed them, and the one that failed is excluded. What the harvest cannot do is tell you how much of each change is due to us, or how common a change is beyond what we found. If the board needs that, we should add a method designed for it before the second phase.
