    Outcome Harvesting, lesson 4 of 8

    From documents to outcome statements

    Steps 2 and 3 are where outcomes are found and written. The harvester first mines documents for potential outcomes, then works with the people who know until each statement is specific enough to verify. Five sections, four rounds to step through, eight questions and one draft to coach.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can extract a potential outcome from a report, and coach its source through two to four rounds to a statement both of you accept.

      RecallFirst, three questions from Lesson 3

      Answer from memory before you read on.

      1Agree the terms and the form before collecting anything

      Before collecting data, harvester and users agree on the instrument, and on what the words in the prime questions mean. The book shows a long exchange with users to pin down what they mean by "rights-based empowerment of women", because the outcomes collected must serve as evidence for that meaning and no other (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 52 to 53).

      The book prefers an instrument that explains itself, laid out as a table that can later be pasted into a database (p. 53). Its example has separate rows for positive and negative outcomes, each with columns for the outcome, its significance, the contribution and the sources (Figure 3.2, p. 55). Putting negative outcomes in their own rows helps them get reported (p. 178).

      2Mine the documents first, and start with the outcome

      Do not skip the documents. Going to people before reading what they have already written risks the coaching relationship (pp. 54 to 55). Ask the intervention's staff to pick the documents most likely to show changes in others: monitoring data, evaluation and field reports, minutes (p. 55). If no such documents exist, skip the step (p. 56).

      Work in a fixed order: the outcome first, then its source with the page number, then the contribution, then the significance. Without an outcome there is no contribution or significance to describe (Figure 3.3, p. 56).

      Reports rarely use the word "outcome" as OH does. Search for its stand-ins, such as achievement, effect, result and consequence (pp. 56 to 57), and apply one test to every candidate:

      Look first for the protagonist of the change. If it is the intervention, it is not an outcome. If it is someone the intervention influenced, it may be.

      Paraphrased from Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 57

      Writing in the active voice, with the actor who changed as the subject, makes the test easy to apply (p. 57). Search without reference to the planned objectives: looking only for what was planned is how unintended and negative outcomes get missed (p. 58).Five drafting tips from the book (pp. 60 to 61): use the source's own words; quantify, or use words such as "few" or "many" when numbers are missing; date both the outcome and the contribution, down to the month, and the contribution must come first; describe contribution, not attribution; spell out acronyms in every cell.

      3Then go to the people closest to the action

      Documents rarely give a complete outcome statement (p. 60). Step 3 fills the gaps and finds outcomes the documents missed, by going to people with the knowledge, the motivation and the time to share what they know (pp. 64 to 65). In the book's experience these are usually field workers rather than managers, and grantees rather than grant makers (p. 64).

      The statements belong to the sources, not the harvester (p. 65). The brief adds two conditions: sources should know from the start that they will be on record, and give consent (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 14); and they should be asked for intended and unintended, positive and negative outcomes (p. 14).

      4Gentle, rigorous ping-pong

      The book calls this step "gentle but rigorous ping-ponging": going back and forth with each source until they affirm, or reject, an outcome statement as specific as it can be (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 65). It usually takes two to four rounds, and rarely more, because sources tire or have other work to put first (p. 67). A survey sent once and used as returned does not produce specific, verifiable outcomes. Goele Scheers, an experienced harvester the book quotes, puts it flatly: "These are never SMART outcomes" (pp. 65 to 66).

      The harvester's role shifts across the rounds (pp. 71 to 78):

      • First round, as a colleague. Send the form already filled with what the documents gave. Ask questions, one per comment, placed exactly where the gap is (pp. 71 to 72).
      • Second round, as an expert. Catch the common errors: an activity written as an outcome, awareness instead of action, carrying on as before instead of a first-time change (pp. 73 to 74).
      • Last round, as a decision maker. Reach agreement on the final wording (p. 78).
      Follow one outcome through the rounds

      Extracted by the harvester from the 2025 annual report, p. 12

      Outcome. Water committees in the district became more active.
      Contribution. We trained committees in record keeping.
      Significance. Not stated.

      The protagonist is the committees, so it may be an outcome. But "more active" is not observable, and nothing is dated or placed.

      Sent to the field officer who wrote the report

      Outcome. Water committees in the district became more active.

      1. Which committees, and how many?
      2. What did they do that they had not done before? Can you describe one thing someone could see?
      3. When did this start: month and year?
      4. Were there committees that did less, or stopped meeting, after the training?

      The officer's reply, edited by the harvester with new questions

      Outcome. Between May and September 2025, seven of the twelve water committees in Example District began collecting a monthly fee and recording it in a cash book.
      Contribution. In March 2025 the project trained committee treasurers in record keeping.

      1. What did the training cover that relates to collecting fees, not just keeping records?
      2. Why does this matter for what the project is trying to achieve?
      3. Where can a reader check this: cash books, minutes, a visit report?

      The officer also reports that two committees stopped meeting after a dispute over the fee. That becomes a separate, negative outcome.

      Agreed by the officer and the harvester

      Outcome. Between May and September 2025, seven of the twelve water committees in Example District began collecting a monthly fee from households and recording it in a cash book.
      Significance. Committees with their own repair money can fix a broken borehole without waiting for the district or the project.
      Contribution. In March 2025 the project trained the treasurers of all twelve committees in setting fees, collecting them and keeping a cash book, and visited each committee once in April to help set its first fee.
      Sources. Field officer, 14 October 2025; committee cash books seen during the October 2025 monitoring visit.

      An invented example, following the order and roles described in Wilson-Grau (2019, pp. 56, 71 to 78).

      5A statement is done when it is specific enough to verify

      The brief's test for a finished statement is SMART (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, pp. 10 to 11):

      LetterWhat the statement must do
      SpecificLet a reader with no special knowledge see who changed what, when and where, and how the change agent contributed
      MeasurableGive objective quantitative and qualitative detail: how much, how many, when, where
      AchievedShow a plausible link between the change agent's actions and the outcome
      RelevantRepresent a significant step towards the impact the change agent seeks
      TimelyFall within the period covered; the contribution may be earlier

      Paraphrased from Wilson-Grau and Britt (2013, pp. 10 to 11). "Achieved" is the brief's word for plausible contribution.

      Not quite

      A harvester accepts as final only statements that contain solid, plausible evidence that could be substantiated, whether or not they are kept confidential (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 14). A vivid story with no date, place or checkable detail does not qualify, however true it may be.

      PracticeApply it

      Five cases. Each asks for a judgement, not a definition. Every answer explains itself.

      PracticeWrite the first ping

      Extracted from an invented field report "Thanks to our advocacy, local leaders are now much more supportive of girls' education, and many schools have improved."

      1. Which leaders: names or roles, and in which villages or district?

      2. What did they do that shows support? For example, did a leader speak at a meeting, change a by-law or bring girls back to school?

      3. When did each of these happen: month and year?

      4. What did your advocacy do, and when, that you think influenced each one?

      5. "Schools have improved" describes conditions, not what someone did. Which actors changed what, in which schools?

      6. Did any leaders oppose girls' education more strongly after your advocacy?
