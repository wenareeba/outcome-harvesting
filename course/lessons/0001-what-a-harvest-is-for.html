    Outcome Harvesting, lesson 1 of 8

    What a harvest is for

    Outcome Harvesting answers some evaluation questions very well and others not at all. This lesson shows what the method does, the situations it was built for, and where it stops. Five short sections, a sorter to try, eight questions and one judgement to write.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can look at an evaluation question and say whether Outcome Harvesting fits it, and what a harvest will and will not tell the people who asked.

      1A harvest starts from what changed and works backwards

      Most evaluations start from the plan. They take the objectives set at the start and measure progress towards them. Outcome Harvesting does not have to. It collects evidence of what has changed, and then works backwards to find out whether, and how, the intervention contributed (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 1).

      Outcome Harvest: the identification, formulation, analysis, and interpretation of outcomes to answer useful questions.

      Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 2

      Two words in that definition carry the rest of the course. Outcomes have a narrow meaning, covered in section 2. Useful questions are the questions the people who will use the harvest need answered; they come first, and everything else follows from them (p. 6).

      The book sets the approach beside evaluation as commonly practised (Wilson-Grau 2019, Table 1.1, p. 4):

      Common practiceOutcome Harvesting
      Tests the planned activities, outputs, outcomes and impactLooks at what was achieved at the level of outcomes, planned or not, then at how the intervention contributed
      The evaluator is seen as independent and objectiveThe evaluator is seen as a facilitator, searching with the implementers for the facts
      Judges success or failureGives evidence of what was achieved and how, and what it means for the intervention
      Checks fidelity to a logic model or planFocuses on what actually happened, intended or not

      Paraphrased from Wilson-Grau (2019, p. 4), who adapts the left-hand column from Doucette (2015). One row on accountability is left out.

      2An outcome is a change in what someone does, not in how well off they are

      In much evaluation writing, an outcome is an improvement in people's lives: better health, more income, more learning. In Outcome Harvesting it means something earlier in the chain. An outcome is a change in what a societal actor does, influenced by the intervention: the actors the intervention set out to influence, not the people who benefit later (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 1 to 2).The brief says "social actor"; the 2019 book mostly says "societal actor". They mean the same: the individual, group, community, organisation or institution that changes (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 4).

      The book's examples come from policy advocacy. A minister publicly commits to limit untendered contracts. Two parties agree to propose transparency legislation together. A legislature passes an anti-corruption law. Each is a change in what someone does (p. 2). The better lives those changes might bring would be the impact, not the outcome (p. 2).

      An outcome can be positive or negative, intended or unintended. What it cannot be is unconnected: there must be a reasonable causal link between the intervention and the change, however small or indirect (p. 2). Lesson 2 is about telling outcomes apart from everything else a report contains.

      3It was built for unpredictable work aimed at social change

      The brief puts the core condition plainly: the method works well when outcomes, rather than activities, are what matters, and it suits complex settings where cause and effect are not well understood (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 2). Advocacy, campaigning and policy work are, in its words, "ideal candidates" (p. 3).

      The book lists the situations in which the approach has proved effective (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 4 to 6):

      SituationWhat it means in practice
      An evaluation is neededFrom ongoing monitoring to developmental, formative or summative evaluation, alone or mixed with other methods
      The focus is on outcomesOutcomes as OH defines them: changes in behaviour, not in beneficiaries' wellbeing
      The intervention aims at social changeInfluencing others to do something new and significantly different
      The context is unpredictableObjectives and theories of change shift over time, and unintended outcomes, including negative ones, are common
      Other approaches are not enoughCounting beneficiaries is unaffordable or cannot show a single initiative's contribution

      Paraphrased from Wilson-Grau (2019, pp. 4 to 6).

      4What a harvest will not tell you

      The method's limits follow from its design. Knowing them early saves a commissioner from asking it the wrong question.

      • It does not measure impact. A harvest can say why an outcome might matter for people's lives, but it does not assess that effect (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 38).
      • It does not judge efficiency or value for money on its own. It speaks to effectiveness, not efficiency or performance (p. 5), and holds no financial data (p. 33).
      • It finds only what its sources know. Outcomes that no informant is aware of are not captured (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 4), so no harvest should claim to be complete (p. 16).
      • It asks a lot of people. Formulating good outcome descriptions takes skill and time, and the harvest depends on those who influenced the outcomes taking part (pp. 3 to 4).
      Not quite

      A report that harvests 200 outcomes and then claims that 200 lives improved has mixed up the two meanings of outcome. The book makes the same point about an advocacy evaluation in India: 488 outcomes did not mean 488 individuals had changed (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 6).

      5Six steps, held together by nine principles

      A harvest runs in six steps, which Lessons 3 to 6 take in turn (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 4). The book adds nine principles that keep a harvest true to the method when the steps are adapted. Five guide the process and four guide the content (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 14 to 15).The book names and numbers these principles in two slightly different ways. This course follows Chapter 1 (pp. 14 to 15). The differences are listed in the bibliography errata.

      Process principlesContent principles
      I. Facilitate usefulness throughout the harvestVI. Harvest social change outcomes
      II. Coach human sources to formulate outcome statementsVII. Formulate an outcome as an observable change
      III. Nurture appropriate participationVIII. Establish plausible influence of the intervention
      IV. Strive for less because it will be more usefulIX. Ensure credible-enough outcomes
      V. Learn Outcome Harvesting experientially

      Wilson-Grau (2019, pp. 14 to 15). The book calls them principles, not rules: they guide choices and must be adapted to context (p. 13).

      PracticeWrite a fit judgement

      Case, invented for practice A foundation has funded a coalition of twelve civil society groups in Uganda for six years to push for better management of public water points. Plans changed often as district politics shifted. The foundation's board asks: "What has the coalition achieved, and is it worth funding for another three years?"

      Outcome Harvesting fits the first half of the question well. The coalition works on advocacy in a setting where plans kept changing, which is where the method was built to work. A harvest would show which district officials, councils and water committees changed what they do, and how the coalition contributed, including changes nobody planned. It will not show whether water services or health improved, which is impact, and it holds no cost data for judging value for money. For the funding decision, the board would need to pair the harvest with cost information and its own view of strategy.
