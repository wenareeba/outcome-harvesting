    Outcome Harvesting, lesson 5 of 8

    Substantiation: credible enough for the uses

    Step 4 asks whether the outcome statements need checking by people independent of the intervention, and if so, which statements, checked by whom, and what counts as passing. The answer is set by the uses, not by a fixed rule. Five sections, six replies to judge, eight questions and one plan to write.

      By the end of this lesson

      You can help users decide whether to substantiate, choose which outcomes and which substantiators, and judge each reply as validated, not validated, or needing a question first.

      1First decide whether to substantiate at all

      The test for Step 4 is whether the whole set of outcomes is credible enough to answer the prime questions for the intended uses (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 88). The book defines the word plainly: "Credibility is the quality of being believed or accepted as true" (p. 88). What users will believe is a social and political judgement as well as a technical one, because different users have different stakes. The book recounts agreeing a substantiation plan with a donor's program officer, only for the donor's evaluation unit to disagree with its own program officer when the findings were presented at headquarters (p. 88).

      "Confirmation of the substance of an Outcome Description by an informant knowledgeable about the outcome, but independent of the change agent."

      Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 18

      Substantiation is not always needed. The book lists reasons why Steps 2 and 3 may already give credible-enough data (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 88 to 89):

      • Users agreed in advance which sources they consider reliable.
      • The sources are the people who know most about the changes.
      • Sources went on record, knowing their information could be checked.
      • The harvester made sure every statement is SMART.
      • The documents were written by someone other than the person who reviewed them in Step 3.
      • Several people agreed each outcome together, so it reflects shared rather than individual knowledge.

      The harvester's first task is to help users decide (p. 89). Against the reasons above, every statement carries the source's own view. In the book's words, "all knowledge is fuzzy; there are only degrees of truth" (p. 89). If the uses need independent confirmation, substantiate. The brief adds that bigger claims of change call for more evidence (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 18).

      2Two purposes, and the right time

      Substantiation can do two things (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 89):

      • Check accuracy. A third party confirms there are no factual errors in the outcome and contribution, or corrects them. For the book's users this has usually been the more important purpose (p. 90).
      • Deepen and broaden understanding. Others add their view of what changed, how the intervention contributed and, above all, why it matters. That can yield quotable comments, ideas for case studies, and the views of allies and participants (p. 100). In one case, four funders paid regional consultants to judge the significance of a network's outcomes, not their accuracy (pp. 100 to 101).

      Timing matters. In a formative or summative evaluation, substantiate once all outcomes are harvested. When outcomes are harvested periodically, as in monitoring or a developmental evaluation, wait until there are enough to judge their relative importance (p. 89).

      3None, some or all: the uses decide

      The book gives three cases from large donors (pp. 90 to 92):

      The pull is towards checking everything, but that is usually too costly to be useful, and even then "you can never be 100% sure of their accuracy" (p. 92). Effort should rise with the importance of the use and how contested the outcomes are (p. 90).

      Users often expect a random sample. The book rejects it as neither practical nor useful for harvests that, in the author's experience, average 97 outcomes, and uses non-probability, purposive, expert sampling instead: outcomes are picked for their importance and their doubtfulness, and the harvester judges who has the expertise to confirm them (p. 93). It quotes Trochim on the weakness of that choice: "even the experts can be, and often are, wrong" (p. 93).The book says a random sample of 97 outcomes at 95% confidence and a ±3% margin would need 51 (p. 93), without stating the proportion it assumed. With the conservative assumption of 50%, the standard formula with a finite population correction gives about 89; 51 follows if about 2.5% of outcomes are expected to be wrong. Either way the sample is over half the harvest, which is the book's point. See the errata page.

      4Choose substantiators who know and stand apart

      The obvious candidates are the actor who changed and the allies who worked with the intervention to influence them. The harvester asks the original sources who else knows, and collects names, roles and contacts (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 93 to 94). The brief adds that donors or allies may choose substantiators, and that a panel of experts can review groups of outcomes (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 18).

      Each substantiator must be independent of the outcome and the contribution, knowledgeable about both, and willing to speak with authority and on the record (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 94). These are judgements, not a checklist. An ally who also contributed may be too close. A minister or chief executive may be hard to reach, so a manager or staff member close to them can be the better choice (p. 94).

      Practical rules from the book's experience:

      • Six or seven in ten people asked agree to respond, so approach at least two per outcome (p. 94). Returns can be lower: in one World Bank pilot, ten people were asked about four outcomes and four replied, covering three, so one outcome went unchecked (World Bank 2014b, p. 19).
      • Ask each substantiator about one outcome, which gives the best return (p. 95).
      • Adapt to language and culture: in one case, grassroots substantiators were interviewed in their own language by a colleague of the same sex (pp. 94 to 95).
      • Promise confidentiality with care. Anyone who knows who was asked about which outcome can work out who said what (p. 97).

      5Judge each reply, then the whole set

      The brief's format presents the final outcome statement and asks the substantiator, for the outcome and then for the contribution, whether they fully agree, partially agree or disagree, with room for comments (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 19). The book adds the significance where it is part of the statement, says to pilot the format with two substantiators, and tells substantiators it will take five to ten minutes (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 95, 97 to 98).

      An outcome is not validated when one independent, knowledgeable and authoritative person considers the description of the outcome, or of the contribution, inaccurate: it did not happen as described, or the intervention did not contribute as described.

      Paraphrased from Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 99

      Replies are rarely that clean. "Fully agree" settles it unless a comment contradicts the score. "Partially agree" or "disagree" may mean a factual objection, which fails the outcome, or simply more detail, which enriches it. When in doubt, ask (pp. 97 to 98). Comments are what make that call possible (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 19).

      Judge each reply

      Outcome: fully agree. Contribution: fully agree. No comment.

      Outcome: fully agree. Comment: "If the council really commits, the budget line will follow."

      Contribution: disagree. Comment: "The council voted on the by-law in February. The project's meeting was in April."

      Outcome: partially agree. Comment: "Correct, and the district engineer also met the committees in June, which helped."

      Outcome: disagree. Comment: "That committee has not met since 2024."

      Outcome: partially agree. Contribution: partially agree. No comment.

      Replies invented for practice. The judgements follow the pages cited in each answer.

      Then judge the whole set. The book's rule of thumb is that at least 90% of the outcomes selected must be validated for the whole set to count as credible enough; outcomes that fail are dropped (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 95). The rule is a starting point, not a formula. In one pilot, 3 of 21 failed, which had been set to trigger checking the other 49. Users and harvesters chose instead to have the two harvesters review the outcomes one by one for SMARTness, because the bigger problem was weak statements across the set (pp. 99 to 100).

      Not quite

      Substantiation is not a vote on whose story wins. When views differ, the harvester records the differences and compares them. The brief calls the process "one of approximation to identify the essential facts of the matter and not one of negotiating differences of opinion" (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, pp. 19 to 20).

      Whatever is decided, the book asks the harvester to make sure that not substantiating is a conscious choice. Users impressed by their SMART statements may see no need, and they may be right, but "you must ensure it is a conscious decision" (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 101). For costing, the book budgets ten to fifteen minutes of each substantiator's time by email, two to three times that of the harvester's, and about three weeks of calendar time (p. 101).

      PracticeWrite a substantiation plan

      An invented case

      A harvest of a three-year advocacy project has produced 48 outcome statements. The primary users are the project team, who want to learn, and the donor, who will use the findings to decide on a second phase. Six outcomes are changes in national policy, which the project's reports claim credit for. Four come from a single partner whose earlier reports were thin. One is a negative outcome the team would rather not publish.

      Whether. Yes, agreed with both users before any outreach, because one use is a funding decision. The donor states what it will find credible, so the plan is not overturned later.

      Which. The six policy outcomes (biggest claims, most important to the decision), the four from the thin-reporting partner (most doubtful), and the negative outcome (to check accuracy before it is reported, not to bury it): 11 of 48.

      Who. For each, names from the original sources: someone in the ministry close to the decision, not the minister, plus an ally who did not co-lead the advocacy. Two approached per outcome, one outcome each.

      How. Email, using the three-point format for outcome and contribution with comments; piloted with two substantiators first; about three weeks.

      Passing. Any outcome a substantiator finds inaccurate is dropped. If fewer than 90% pass, users decide whether to check more or to review all 48 for SMARTness.
