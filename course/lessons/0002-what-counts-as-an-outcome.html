    Outcome Harvesting, lesson 2 of 8

    What counts as an outcome

    Most errors in a harvest start here: an activity or an attitude written up as an outcome, or a contribution that claims too much. This lesson sets the line between what counts and what does not, and shows how to write an outcome statement that holds up. Five sections, a builder to try, eight questions and one rewrite.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can tell an outcome from an output, an attitude change or an impact, and write one that names who changed what, when and where, and how the intervention plausibly contributed.

      1Five kinds of change in behaviour count

      The book defines the social change that a harvest looks for in one sentence, as part of its sixth principle:

      Social change in Outcome Harvesting is defined as societal actors modifying the way they do things in new and significant ways (outcome) that the intervention plausibly influenced (contribution).

      Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 170

      It then names the five kinds of behavioural change that count (p. 170). The examples in the right-hand column are this course's, set in one district.

      KindWhat changesExample
      ActionAn actor does something different, onceThe district chair publicly backs community water committees
      ActivityAn actor does something different, over timeThe chair raises water committees at every council meeting
      RelationshipTwo or more actors change how they deal with each otherThe water office and the committees agree to meet each quarter
      PolicyA collective actor changes its rules, norms or lawsThe council adopts a by-law on borehole repair funds
      PracticeA collective actor acts on a new policyThe water office releases repair funds each quarter, as the by-law requires

      Two limits follow. Doing more of the same, or doing it better, usually does not count unless another actor influenced the decision; an outcome is a qualitative change, something new (p. 171). And delivering a service is not, on its own, social change. Treating a patient meets a need; persuading people to change what they do is the territory of a harvest (p. 171).

      2Knowledge and attitudes are not outcomes until behaviour changes

      Development reports often pair "attitudes and behaviour" as if they were the same thing. The book argues they are not, and that changes in knowledge, attitudes or skills do not reliably predict a change in what people do (p. 175). Its rule is simple: accept only demonstrated changes as outcomes (p. 175). The brief lists the same trap among the common shortcomings: awareness, knowledge and commitment need evidence of a change in behaviour (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 16).

      Context can move the line. If men in a deeply patriarchal setting had to be persuaded even to attend a workshop on women's rights, their attending may itself be an outcome. Where officials attend such workshops as a matter of routine, their attendance is an output of the workshop, and the outcome would be what they do next (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 176).

      ResultWhat it isWhy
      The project trained 40 council staffOutputSomething the project did
      Staff say they understand repair budgeting betterChange in knowledgeNot yet a change in what they do
      The council put repair costs into its budgetOutcomeA change in what a societal actor does
      Fewer families walk over an hour for waterImpactA change in people's lives (p. 171)

      3Not acting, and getting worse, can both be outcomes

      Persuading an actor not to do something can be a significant outcome, though it is awkward to write as a change. The fix is to give the context: an official persuaded not to follow a trend in other ministries has changed course (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 176).

      Negative outcomes count too: changes the intervention influenced that undermine what it is trying to achieve (p. 177). The book gives three reasons sources leave them out: they do not know of them, they think the positive ones matter more, or they fear blame (p. 177). In some kinds of change, things get worse before they get better, so a harvest taken halfway can look very different from one taken at the end (pp. 177 to 178).The brief makes the same point from the other side: if a source reports only positive outcomes, either the claims are not credible or the change agent is not taking enough risks (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 14).

      4Every outcome needs a plausible contribution, not proof

      A change in behaviour is only half an outcome statement. The other half describes how the intervention plausibly influenced it (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 2). The book sets two tests (p. 178):

      1. Plausible enough for the uses. The primary user, the harvester and any substantiator see a reasonable, probable link between what the intervention did and the change.
      2. Specific enough to verify. The description of the contribution is concrete and measurable enough that someone could check it.

      The contribution can be direct or indirect, intended or not, and is usually partial. Research the intervention produced, which an ally then used to lobby for a policy change, contributed indirectly (p. 179). Influence can run from inspiring and encouraging, through facilitating and supporting, to persuading and pressuring (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 10).

      Not quite

      "The project's training led to the new by-law" claims attribution. Interventions are rarely the sole reason an actor changes; in most cases they contribute partly or indirectly (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 16). Write what the intervention did, and let the reader judge the link.

      5Write it as one statement with three parts

      The book's outcome statement has three parts, each one or two sentences (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 38 to 39):

      • Outcome: when, who, what they did differently, and where.
      • Significance: why the change matters for what the intervention is trying to achieve.
      • Contribution: how the intervention influenced it: who did what with whom.

      Where one change ends and the next begins is a judgement. The book's rule of thumb: keep different behaviours apart, and group dates, actors and places according to how significant the outcomes are (p. 173).The World Bank's field guide uses a narrower definition: changes in the people, groups, communities or organisations a programme works with directly, its "sphere of influence" (World Bank 2014a, p. 9). The brief credits the original definition to Outcome Mapping (Wilson-Grau and Britt 2013, p. 1, note 1).

      Build an outcome statement

        The checks prompt you; they do not grade. They look for a named actor, a date, a place, wording that sounds like knowledge or attitude, and wording that claims attribution. The example is invented.

        PracticeRewrite a report sentence as an outcome

        From an invented progress report "In 2025 the project trained 25 health unit management committee members in Example District on community scorecards. Participants reported much greater awareness of their oversight role. In February 2026 two committees used scorecards to question the in-charge about drug stock-outs, and the district health office then moved its drug deliveries to monthly."

        Outcome. In February 2026, two health unit management committees in Example District used community scorecards to question health unit in-charges about drug stock-outs.

        Significance. Committees holding their own health units to account is the oversight role the scorecards were meant to build.

        Contribution. In 2025 the project trained 25 committee members, including members of both committees, to use community scorecards.

        The district health office's move to monthly deliveries is a second outcome, with a separate statement. The training and the reported awareness are not outcomes.
