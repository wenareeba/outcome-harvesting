OH among related approaches: choosing and combining

Outcome Harvesting has a parent, a sibling, a cousin and a neighbour that starts from the other end. Knowing them tells you when to lead with a harvest, when to combine it, and when another approach fits the question better. Five sections, five evaluation questions to match, eight questions and one design to write.

By the end of this lesson

You can say how Outcome Harvesting differs from Outcome Mapping, Most Significant Change, QuIP and contribution analysis, and design an evaluation that combines a harvest with one of them.

      01Where the method comes from

      The book names three pillars. Outcome Mapping gave the concepts: an intervention's sphere of influence, and outcomes as changes in behaviour that represent social change. Utilization-Focused Evaluation gave the process: primary intended users and their uses, throughout. Professional evaluation standards gave the third (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 18). In the book's own summary, the six steps are "methodologically based on Utilization-Focused Evaluation and conceptually on Outcome Mapping" (p. 26).

      The book also places the method among goal-free approaches: ones that identify what has been achieved without starting from what was planned (p. 23). That starting point is what separates it from most of its neighbours.

      02Outcome Mapping: the parent

      "An approach to planning and monitoring that puts people at the center, defines outcomes as changes in behavior and helps measure contribution to complex change processes."

      Wilson-Grau 2019, glossary, p. 215

      The difference is where each starts. Outcome Mapping is mainly a planning method: in the book's account, seven of its twelve steps plan an intervention, four monitor it, and only the last concerns evaluation, as a plan (p. 18). Its monitoring tracks "progress markers", agreed in advance, in the actors a program works with directly, its "boundary partners" (p. 193). Outcome Harvesting kept the concepts and dropped the planning. It looks for change in any actor the intervention influenced, planned or not, and its origins lie partly in networks that found Outcome Mapping's monitoring too unwieldy (pp. 18, 193).This section describes Outcome Mapping through Outcome Harvesting sources. The Outcome Mapping manual itself (Earl, Carden and Smutylo 2001) is in this course's verification queue and is not yet cited directly.

      03Most Significant Change: the sibling

      MSC is participatory monitoring and evaluation built on stories. It involves "the collection of significant change (SC) stories emanating from the field level, and the systematic selection of the most significant of these stories by panels of designated stakeholders or staff" (Davies and Dart 2005, p. 8). It uses no predefined indicators (p. 8), and the discussion of why one change matters more than another is part of the point: it surfaces the values an organisation holds (p. 12).

      The two are close. USAID's note on complexity-aware monitoring lists both among five recommended approaches (Britt 2013, p. 7), calls both goal-free and indicator-free (p. 14), and draws the main line between them: "Outcome Harvesting, however, puts more emphasis than MSC on verification and on identifying and describing contribution" (p. 12).

      They also suit different jobs. MSC's authors say its benefits may not justify its cost for a retrospective evaluation of a finished program or a report for accountability (Davies and Dart 2005, p. 13). Those are jobs a harvest can do: OH needs no plan or theory of change from the start (INTRAC 2017, p. 4), and has been used retrospectively on a small budget (Beardmore, Jones and Seal 2023, p. 6).

      04QuIP: the cousin

      Copestake, who designed and piloted the Qualitative Impact Protocol, finds the two share their philosophy: both work back from outcomes to the activities that may have caused them, both suit complex settings, and both seek credible evidence of contribution rather than precise attribution (Copestake 2017, before the comparison table).

      The differences, in his reading (Copestake 2017, after the comparison table):

      • QuIP is narrower: it focuses on the feedback of intended beneficiaries, in a way that is easier for third parties to audit.
      • QuIP builds in bias controls, such as blindfolding and separating data collection from analysis.
      • OH is broader, covering monitoring as well as impact, and seems more tailored to advocacy, campaigning and policy work.

      He concludes that "it would be more accurate to describe QuIP as a form of OH than the other way round", and calls them "mutually affirming approaches that belong to a broad family of more qualitative and interpretive approaches to assessing change" (Copestake 2017, after the comparison table).

      05Contribution analysis: starting from the other end

      Contribution analysis starts with a theory. Mayne puts it plainly: "The basis of the contribution claim is the empirical evidence confirming a solid ToC of an intervention" (Mayne 2019, p. 173). Its steps run from setting the cause-and-effect questions, through developing the theory of change and gathering evidence on its results and assumptions, to assessing and strengthening the contribution claim (p. 173). The book lists contribution analysis among methods that require theories of change or other predefined results, and turns instead to reasoning that, like a detective's, starts from the effect and works back to the cause (Wilson-Grau 2019, pp. 181 to 182).

      The sources do not agree

      Classifications differ. Mayne lists Wilson-Grau and Britt's brief among theory-based methods (Mayne 2019, p. 172), while the book calls the method goal-free (Wilson-Grau 2019, p. 23). INTRAC groups contribution analysis with tools that start from change and work backwards (INTRAC 2017, p. 4), while Mayne starts it from a theory of change. Labels like these are shorthand; check what a method actually starts from.

      The two combine well. BetterEvaluation describes Outcome Harvesting as able to complement theory-based approaches that focus on planned change, by finding the unintended changes and their importance to users (Wilson-Grau et al. 2024, design section). One reasonable design: a harvest first, to find what changed; then contribution analysis on the few outcomes that matter most, where the theory of change makes a stronger claim worth testing. Both rest on behaviour change, which Mayne, citing Outcome Mapping, calls a key focus for most interventions (Mayne 2019, p. 178).

      Which approach should lead?

      "Which policy changes, expected or not, did our five-year advocacy campaign influence, and how?"

      "We want field staff and communities to discuss, every quarter, which changes they value most, and why."

      "Our nutrition program has a detailed theory of change. Did it contribute to mothers adopting new feeding practices, and through which pathway?"

      "The funder wants farmers' own accounts of what changed in their lives and why, gathered so that they do not know who commissioned the study."

      "The program ended last year. There was no theory of change and little monitoring. What did it achieve?"

      Questions invented for practice. In real evaluations, approaches are often combined; this asks only which should lead.

      PracticeDesign a combination

      An invented brief

      A four-year governance program has a theory of change with one central pathway: training district officials leads to more transparent budgets. The funder asks two questions. What did the program change, including anything unexpected? And how far did the training pathway contribute to the budget changes it claims?

      First, a harvest to answer the first question. Documents and staff give potential outcomes in any actor the program influenced, including negative and unplanned ones; field staff and district officials refine them to SMART statements; a sample of the most important and most doubtful is substantiated. Then, contribution analysis on the budget outcomes only. The theory of change sets out the training pathway and its assumptions; the harvest supplies verified outcomes and their contribution stories as evidence; other influences, such as a new national budget law, are examined as part of the claim. The harvest comes first because it may show that budgets changed for reasons the theory did not anticipate, which the contribution analysis must then account for. Users agree both designs at the start, so the second step is not a surprise.
