# Outcome Harvesting red team protocol

A checklist for testing whether an Outcome Harvest followed the method and whether its claims match its evidence. Use it on your own harvest before sign-off, on a report you are reviewing, or on a harvest design before data collection starts.

It follows the six steps in Wilson-Grau and Britt (2013), the **Brief**, and the guidance in Wilson-Grau (2019), the **Book**. Page numbers refer to one or the other, as marked.

## How to use it

1. Work through the steps in order. For each check, record **Pass**, **Partial**, **Fail** or **Not applicable**, with the page or section of the report that shows it.
2. A **Fail** on any check marked **(critical)** means the report should not be called an Outcome Harvest as it stands, or its main claim must be narrowed.
3. Finish with the five verification questions at the end. Name at least one external check for each audit: a source document opened at the cited page, a substantiator contacted, or a second reader.
4. Be fair. Weak checks often reflect limits of time, money and access, not carelessness. The test is whether the claims match the evidence, not whether the harvesters did everything possible.

## Step 1. Design the harvest

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **(critical)** Primary intended users are named, with the decisions or actions the harvest will inform | Named people or roles, and their principal uses of the findings and the process | Book pp. 31, 33 to 34 |
| 1.2 | **(critical)** Prime harvesting questions were written down and agreed before data collection | The questions appear in the report or its annexes, and the findings refer back to them | Book p. 34; Brief p. 6 |
| 1.3 | The contents of an outcome statement were agreed: outcome and contribution, usually significance, and any extra fields with a reason | A stated format, ideally with the real example used to agree it | Book pp. 38 to 42 |
| 1.4 | Users and harvesters agreed early on which data and sources would make the findings credible enough | A stated standard of credibility tied to the uses | Brief p. 8 |
| 1.5 | Categories for analysis were agreed, usually from the prime questions | The categories used in Step 5 match the design | Brief p. 8 |
| 1.6 | The timing fits how predictable the results are | Earlier harvests where results were uncertain, or a reason why not | Brief pp. 8 to 9; Book p. 44 |

## Step 2. Review documentation and draft outcome descriptions

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Documents were reviewed before sources were asked to add outcomes | A list of documents reviewed, before interviews or surveys | Book p. 42; Brief p. 10 |
| 2.2 | **(critical)** Each outcome is a change in what a social actor does (behaviour, relationships, actions, activities, policies or practices), not an activity or output of the intervention | No outcome of the form "the project trained..." or "a workshop was held..." | Brief pp. 2, 10 |
| 2.3 | Draft descriptions follow the level of detail agreed in Step 1 | Statements of similar length and content | Brief p. 10; Book p. 42 |

## Step 3. Engage with change agents to formulate outcome descriptions

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Change agents were helped to understand what counts as an outcome before formulating them | A briefing, guidance note or worked example | Brief p. 13 |
| 3.2 | Drafts went back and forth with the sources until agreed | Evidence of revision rounds, or the final agreed versions | Brief p. 13; Book p. 65 |
| 3.3 | **(critical)** Sources were asked for unintended as well as intended outcomes, and negative as well as positive ones | The question appears in the instrument, and the report says what came back | Brief p. 14 |
| 3.4 | Sources knew they were on record and gave consent; any confidential versions are explained | A stated consent process and treatment of confidential outcomes | Brief p. 14 |
| 3.5 | Outcome statements are specific, measurable, show a plausible contribution, are relevant and fall within the period | Who, what, when, where, and how the change agent contributed | Brief pp. 10 to 11 |
| 3.6 | Changes in awareness, knowledge or commitment count only with evidence of a change in behaviour | No outcome that is only "increased awareness" | Brief p. 16 |

## Step 4. Substantiate

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | **(critical)** Whether to substantiate was a conscious decision made with the users, and the report says why | A stated decision and reason, whichever way it went | Book pp. 89, 101 |
| 4.2 | If some outcomes were substantiated, the report says how they were chosen | For example the most important, the most questionable or the most contested | Book pp. 90, 92 to 93 |
| 4.3 | **(critical)** Substantiators knew about the outcome and were independent of the change agent | Role and relationship of each substantiator, at least in summary | Brief p. 18; Book p. 94 |
| 4.4 | Substantiators were asked about the outcome and the contribution separately, with room for comment | A record of agreement for each part | Brief p. 19 |
| 4.5 | Outcomes a substantiator found did not happen as described were excluded from the evidence | The number excluded, and confirmation they were not used | Book p. 95 |
| 4.6 | Differing views were recorded, not negotiated away | Disagreements noted and compared | Brief pp. 19 to 20 |

## Step 5. Analyse and interpret

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | **(critical)** The findings answer the prime harvesting questions | Each question answered from the outcomes, with the clusters that support it | Brief p. 21; Book p. 112 |
| 5.2 | The level of analysis suits the questions: each outcome, one change agent, or a programme | A stated level | Brief pp. 21 to 22 |
| 5.3 | Outcomes are classified with the agreed categories, mostly one category each | A classification table or database | Brief p. 8; Book p. 112 |
| 5.4 | The interpretive lens is named | For example the theory of change, the strategy, or the harvesters' expertise | Brief p. 23 |

## Step 6. Support use of findings

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | The findings reached the users in a form they can act on | A report and a presentation or workshop with the users | Brief p. 25 |
| 6.2 | Recommendations stay within what the harvesters can know; otherwise discussion points are offered | No firm recommendation that depends on facts the harvesters did not have | Brief p. 25 |
| 6.3 | The harvest has a real use, not a symbolic one | Users engaged in decisions throughout, not only at the end | Book p. 140 |

## The claims

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| C.1 | **(critical)** The report claims contribution, not attribution | "Contributed to" or "influenced", not "caused", "led to" or "was responsible for", unless argued | Brief p. 16 |
| C.2 | Bigger claims rest on more evidence and substantiation | The outcomes behind the main claims were substantiated, or the report says why not | Brief p. 18 |
| C.3 | The report does not claim impact on people's lives from outcome data alone | Impact language limited to what the outcomes show | Book p. 38 |
| C.4 | The report says that only outcomes known to the sources were captured | A stated limitation; no claim to have found every outcome | Brief pp. 4, 16 |
| C.5 | A set of only positive outcomes is treated as a warning sign, not as success | Discussion of why no negative outcomes appeared | Brief pp. 14, 17 |

## Five verification questions

Each one can expose a real error. Answer all five.

1. **Success bias.** How many reported outcomes are unintended or negative? If none, does the report explain why, and was the question asked (Brief pp. 14, 17)?
2. **Independent substantiation.** Pick one substantiated outcome. Was the substantiator outside the change agent organisation, and well placed to know what happened (Brief p. 18)?
3. **Outcome wording.** Pick five outcomes at random. Does each name a social actor and an observable change in what they do (Brief pp. 2, 16; Book p. 1)?
4. **Contribution overreach.** Search the summary and conclusions for "caused", "led to", "resulted in" and "because of". Is each backed by a stated contribution and, where the claim is large, by substantiation (Brief pp. 16, 18)?
5. **Trace one outcome to its source.** Pick one outcome and open the document or interview record it cites. Does the source say what the statement says?

## Verdict

- **A credible Outcome Harvest.** No critical check fails; the claims match the evidence.
- **Credible with narrowed claims.** A critical check fails on part of the harvest; the report holds once its main claims are limited to the outcomes that pass.
- **Not an Outcome Harvest as reported.** Critical checks fail across the harvest, for example no prime questions, outcomes that are outputs, or claims of attribution.
